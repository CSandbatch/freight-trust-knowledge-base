INTRODUCTION 
The American Transportation Research Institute’s Analysis of the Operational Costs of Trucking 
report is the industry’s leading public benchmarking tool for costs, revenue, and key 
performance metrics across primary fleet operations.  The data in this report represents 
operational costs for the year 2025, as well as leading cost indicators for Q1 2026. 
Freight markets experienced recessionary conditions in 2025 for the third year in a row while 
broader economic conditions became more uncertain amid the implementation, adjustment, and 
revocation of tariffs.1  Gross domestic product (GDP) fluctuated amid these conditions, ranging 
from -0.6 percent in Q1 to +4.4 percent in Q3, for an annual increase of 2.1 percent.2  
Consumer inflation slowed slightly from 2.9 percent in 2024 to 2.7 percent in 2025.3 
Freight demand remained stagnant throughout 2025 in most key sectors.  Annual housing 
construction starts fell by 1 percent in 2025, deepening a four-year slide; manufacturing output 
rose by 1 percent but remained well below pre-pandemic levels.4  Retail sales rose by 3.8 
percent, though this was just 1.1 percentage points above inflation.5  As a result, trucking rates 
and tonnage remained flat compared to 2024.6  Faced with sluggish demand and rising costs, 
freight capacity left the market steadily in 2025.7 
In 2025, the average operational costs of trucking rose from $2.260 per mile in 2024 to a record 
$2.336 per mile, an increase of 3.4 percent.  Excluding fuel, the operational costs of trucking 
rose by 4.2 percent in 2025, from $1.779 to $1.854.  This rate of increase was still 1.5 
percentage points above inflation during the same period and 0.6 percentage points above last 
year’s increase, indicating an uptick in inflationary pressure in trucking.

METHODOLOGY 
This report pertains to for-hire combination truck operations in 2025.  All motor carriers are 
welcome to participate, with outreach conducted through direct contacts, trade press, and trade 
organizations.  Data were collected directly and confidentially from for-hire motor carriers from 
February 2026 through May 2026 via online and PDF submission, with Confidentiality 
Agreements provided as requested.  Data quality is ensured through robust reviews, participant 
follow-ups, a high rate of returning participants, and corroboration with external sources (Table 
1).

Three new questions were added to the data collection form in 2026:  
1. h ours-of-service (HOS) utilization;  
2. in-cab technology spend per truck; and  
3. total litigation expense (verdicts, settlements, and/or legal costs).   
In addition, truck and trailer costs, which previously had been reported as a single category, 
were collected as two separate categories.  The full data collection form – including specific 
data reporting instructions and definitions – can be found in Appendix A.  Note that owner-
operators (OOs) use a streamlined version of the form. 
Industry-wide averages in the report are calculated by weighting responses by each sector’s 
market share based on the BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW).  Table 2 
compares respondent truck counts to BLS employment by sector.  Less-than-Truckload (LTL) 
fleets were overrepresented in the sample, and truckload fleets were underrepresented.  The 
“specialized” sector in this report, as in BLS and other federal sources, refers to a broad group 
of sectors that include flatbed, tanker, refrigerated, intermodal, and bulk.

RESPONDENT DEMOGRAPHICS 
In 2025, 65.2 percent of U.S. DOT-registered carriers operated on a for-hire basis exclusively, 
with 26.2 percent registered as private fleets and 6.9 percent engaged in both for-hire and 
private carriage.10 
The findings in this report represent 182,248 combination trucks, 5.2 percent of all registered 
combination trucks.11  These truck-tractors ran a total of 14.67 billion miles, 7.6 percent of all 
U.S. combination truck vehicle miles traveled (VMT).12  As such, the benchmarks in this report 
represent a meaningful share of the for-hire trucking industry in the U.S. and Canada.

Size of Operations 
Respondent demographics were consistent with previous years’ reports.  The LTL sector 
represented the largest share of trucks in the data, while the truckload sector represented the 
largest share of carriers (Table 2).  Fleets with 251 to 1,000 trucks made up the largest share of 
respondents (Figure 1).  While there was a year-over-year increase in OO participation, fleets 
with 5 to 25 trucks made up a smaller share of participants in the 2026 study.  U.S Department 
of Transportation (U.S. DOT) data shows that 91.5 percent of fleets operate with 10 or fewer 
trucks.13

OPERATIONAL COST FINDINGS 
The operational cost of trucking in 2025 was $2.336, an increase of 7.6 cents or 3.4 percent 
over 2024’s total.  There was no single contributor to cost increases in 2025, as every line-item 
aside from permits and licenses increased year-over-year.  Excluding fuel, which increased by 
just 0.1 cents, the operational costs of trucking rose from $1.779 to $1.854 (4.2%).  Table 8 
details per-mile costs by line-item over the past decade.  Every line-item except fuel and permits 
reached a record high in 2025. 
On a per-hour basis, the operational cost of trucking in 2025 was $106.69 with fuel and $84.68 
without fuel.  Table 9 details per-hour costs by line-item over the past decade.

CONCLUSIONS 
In 2025 the average marginal costs of trucking were $2.336 per mile – 3.4 percent higher than 
in 2024.  Excluding fuel, costs rose by 4.2 percent to $1.854 per mile.  This was the first year 
since the pandemic in which costs rose at a higher rate than the previous year, signaling the 
expansion of inflationary pressures. 
Leading cost increases in 2025 were tolls (13.2%), R&M (8.6%), driver benefits (6.6%), and tires 
(6.4%).  There were few areas of cost relief.  Of the core marginal costs, only fuel and driver pay 
rose at rates below annual inflation (2.7%).  Though truck and trailer procurement costs rose by 
a moderate 3.6 percent, this was largely due to deferred replacement of assets.  Indeed, fleets 
were forced to make cost-saving compromises that resulted in older average truck ages, fewer 
miles between breakdowns, higher driver turnover, and reduced non-driver headcounts 
compared to the previous year. 
Motor carriers reduced effective capacity – the number of trucks hauling freight – by 5.5 percent 
in 2025 via a combination of reducing fleet sizes outright (-2.4%) and leaving a significant share 
of their remaining trucks unseated (10%).  This represented, by far, the largest reduction in 
freight capacity among existing carriers since the start of the freight recession. 
The industry’s austerity efforts did achieve some success.  The truckload, refrigerated, and 
tanker sectors made modest gains in profitability – with the truckload sector recovering from a 
negative average operating margin in 2024 – but of these only the tanker sector had an 
operating margin of 1 percent or higher.  The flatbed sector averaged an operating loss of -0.5 
percent.  Only LTLs and fleets with more than 1,000 trucks had healthy margins in 2025, but 
without any year-over-year improvement. 
Economic conditions in the trucking industry provided some areas for cautious optimism in 
2026.  Real GDP rose by 2.1 percent in Q1 2026 – in line with the overall growth rate in 2025 – 
though over half of this increase (55%) was attributable to low-freight sectors of the economy: 
information, federal government, and professional, scientific, and technical services.65  The 
inflation rate stood at 4.2 percent in May 2026, largely due to high energy costs following the 
U.S. attack on Iran.  Much of this inflationary pressure should dissipate if hostilities can be 
resolved even though normal oil shipping in the Strait of Hormuz itself is not expected to recover 
until 2027.66   
Freight rates rose steadily over the first half of 2026, generating cause for optimism that the 
three-year post-pandemic freight recession may finally be ending.  DAT and U.S. Bank data 
showed that in May of 2026 truckload dry van spot rates were up 31 percent and contract rates 
were up 9 percent year-over-year.67  The same report also showed, however, that volumes in 
both freight categories fell over 15 percent.  ATA’s tonnage index showed a similar though less
